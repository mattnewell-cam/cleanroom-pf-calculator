How it is calculated

Weather comes from 5 years of hourly records (2019–2023) at the airports of the 60 largest US metros. For every record (2-minute mean wind speed and direction at 10 m, and outdoor temperature) the model works out how much unfiltered air gets in, as a fraction f of the fan's filtered flow, and averages f over the period.

  1. Airport wind is converted to wind at 6 m in the chosen terrain (×0.93 open, ×0.64 suburban; ASHRAE Fundamentals ch. 24).
  2. Each wall's pressure is Cp(angle) × ½ρU², with Cp for the surroundings from AIVC Technical Note 44. Gusts add a random part with rms 0.2–0.5 × ½ρU² (An & Jung 2023), independent for each wall and the roof.
  3. The rest of the house settles at the pressure that balances its own leaks (walls 60%, ceiling 25%, floor 15%), including stack effect from the indoor–outdoor temperature difference.
  4. The fan draws outdoor air through the room's outside wall and pushes it through the filter into the room. Its calm-weather flow is what holds the chosen overpressure. A constant-flow fan keeps that flow whatever happens. A centrifugal or axial fan follows its curve, Pfan = Pmax(1 − Q/Qfree)k with k = 1 or 2, sized to run at half its maximum pressure in calm weather. Its flow rises when wind pushes on its intake and falls when the room's pressure rises against it. The filter's pressure drop scales with flow. The fan's air leaves through the outside-wall leaks and the house-side leaks, with Q = C·Δp0.65 for each. The room pressure is solved for each gust. Air comes in wherever the outside is at higher pressure than the room. House air counts as outdoor air. The whole-house case uses a constant-flow fan.
  5. Room concentration / outdoor = (Pfilter + f) / (1 + f + (CADR + k·V)/Q). Protection factor is its inverse.

A room with a fixed-flow fan rises in pressure when wind pushes on its outside wall. If a share φ of the leaks is in that wall, nothing gets in through it until the wall is (1−φ)−1/0.65 times the calm overpressure above the house interior: 1.0× at φ = 1%, 2.9× at 50%, 34× at 90%. But the amount that gets in once that happens scales with φ. So infiltration peaks at a middle share and falls toward both ends: a hardened outside wall (small φ) lets little in because it has few leaks, and a room whose leaks are almost all in the outside wall (large φ) rides up and down with that wall. Near the ends, effects the model leaves out (pressure differences inside the house, pressure varying along the wall, gusts faster than about 1 s) will set the real limit. A whole house gets far less of the riding-up effect, so it needs about three times the overpressure of a room. Not included: fans whose flow falls as room pressure rises, door openings, leaks on the suction side of the fan, and the wind on the fan's own intake. No one has measured this under real wind at PF 1000. The only direct test (NIOSH, Heitbrink et al. 2000) is consistent with the model. Weather data: NOAA Integrated Surface Database (ISD-lite), 2019–2023. Flow exponent 0.65 (Chan et al. 2013).
